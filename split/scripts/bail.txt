# 1 · THREE SITUATIONS
10 | Three clients call you today. One is already in custody. One fears arrest. And one stays detained while investigators miss their deadline. Which bail do you seek for each?
10 | Under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, or B N S S, the situation decides the route: regular, anticipatory or default bail.
# 2 · REGULAR BAIL
8 | Client in custody? Regular bail usually means seeking release after arrest, or after surrender into the court's custody.
8 | Bailable offence? Section Four Seventy Eight generally makes release a right, once its requirements are met.
8 | Non bailable offence? Section Four Eighty governs bail before the Magistrate, subject to restrictions and exceptions. || Non-bailable offence? Section 480 governs bail before the Magistrate, subject to restrictions and exceptions.
10 | Section Four Eighty Three gives bail powers to the Sessions Court and the High Court. And remember this: non bailable does not mean bail is impossible.
8 | What does the court weigh? The accusation, the available material, and risks such as absconding or influencing witnesses.
# 3 · ARJUN IS ARRESTED
10 | Take Arjun, arrested in a non bailable case. You seek regular bail. He has a fixed address, he will attend court, and he will not contact witnesses.
8 | The prosecution explains its custody concerns. The court then weighs all the circumstances and the legal restrictions.
8 | Bail granted? Then Arjun must meet the release requirements and obey every condition. Remember, bail does not decide guilt.
# 4 · ANTICIPATORY BAIL
6 | No arrest yet, but your client fears one? Anticipatory bail is protection before arrest.
10 | Under Section Four Eighty Two, someone who reasonably apprehends arrest for a non bailable offence may approach the Sessions Court or the High Court.
6 | The court can direct that, if arrested on that accusation, your client be released on bail.
8 | Common mistake alert! Merely filing the application gives your client no automatic protection. The court's order does.
10 | Watch the exclusions. Section Four Eighty Two, sub section Four, excludes accusations under B N S Section Sixty Five and Section Seventy, sub section Two.
8 | These are specified sexual offences against minor girls. And some special laws also bar anticipatory bail, so check them first.
# 5 · MEERA'S CASE
10 | How do you protect Meera? She faces a non bailable accusation and has concrete reasons to fear arrest. She applies under Section Four Eighty Two.
10 | Protection granted? The court may require her to attend police questioning, avoid threatening witnesses, and take the court's permission before leaving India.
10 | Anticipatory bail protects liberty while the case proceeds. It does not cancel the F I R, or excuse cooperation with a lawful investigation.
# 6 · DEFAULT BAIL
8 | Route three runs on a clock. Default bail, also called statutory bail, arises under Section One Eighty Seven, sub section Three.
8 | It applies when the charge sheet is not filed within the applicable period of detention during investigation.
10 | The section specifies ninety days for offences punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment for a term of ten years or more.
8 | For other offences, the section specifies sixty days. Your client must claim the right, and be prepared to furnish bail.
8 | Before you pick the deadline, check three things: the exact offence, the judicial interpretation and any special law.
# 7 · WHERE COUNTING BEGINS
8 | Where does the clock start? It does not start at the F I R date. It starts at the first remand order, and that day counts as day one.
8 | That is the Supreme Court's counting rule. And moving from police custody to judicial custody does not restart the clock.
10 | These sixty or ninety days concern detention during investigation. The trial need not end within them. Prolonged undertrial detention has separate rules. || These 60 or 90 days concern detention during investigation. The trial need not end within them. Prolonged undertrial detention has separate rules.
# 8 · A 60-DAY EXAMPLE
10 | Count with me. Ravi's case is in the sixty day category, with no special law extension. His first remand is on the first of January, twenty twenty six. || Count with me. Ravi's case is in the 60-day category, with no special law extension. His first remand is on 1 January 2026.
8 | Mark that as day one. January accounts for thirty one days. February adds twenty eight, taking the count to fifty nine. || Mark that as day one. January accounts for 31 days. February adds 28, taking the count to 59.
8 | The first of March is day sixty. No police report filed by then? The right arises on the second of March, day sixty one. || 1 March is day 60. No police report filed by then? The right arises on 2 March, day 61.
10 | Ravi can then claim default bail and offer to furnish bail. But he does not simply walk out: he needs a court order and compliance with the release requirements.
# 9 · WHY A TIMELY CLAIM MATTERS
8 | Timing matters. Who moves first, your claim or the charge sheet? Suppose Ravi claims default bail as soon as the period expires.
10 | Right then, no charge sheet has been filed, and he offers to furnish bail. A later charge sheet cannot defeat his claim, just because the court has not decided it yet.
10 | But if a valid charge sheet is filed before he claims the right, default bail is ordinarily unavailable. He may still seek regular bail.
10 | Be very clear: default bail rests on the missed statutory deadline. It is not an acquittal, and the investigation may continue after release.
# 10 · REMEMBER
10 | Three questions to remember. Already in custody? Consider regular bail. Reasonably fearing arrest? Consider anticipatory bail.
10 | Investigation deadline expired without the required report? Examine default bail immediately. Know the stage, check the section, act in time!
