# 1 · FROM FIR TO JUDGMENT
10 | Your client is accused of murder. He denies it. Does the F I R lead straight to punishment? No. Evidence must be presented, tested and considered.
8 | Call him Arjun. Follow his contested Sessions trial under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, or B N S S.
# 2 · BEFORE THE TRIAL
8 | Investigation or trial? Keep them separate. Police investigate, and submit their report under Section One Ninety Three.
6 | Under Section Two Ten, the Magistrate decides whether to take cognizance of the offence.
10 | Section Two Thirty requires the papers to be supplied free, within fourteen days from the accused's production or appearance. Check both dates carefully.
8 | Is the offence triable exclusively by the Court of Session? Then the case is committed there, under Section Two Thirty Two.
8 | The Public Prosecutor then opens the case under Section Two Forty Nine, explaining the accusation and the evidence.
# 3 · DISCHARGE, CHARGE, PLEA
8 | Can your client be discharged? Under Section Two Fifty, the application may be made within sixty days from commitment.
6 | Insufficient ground for proceeding? Then the Judge must discharge the accused, and record reasons.
10 | Otherwise, Section Two Fifty One requires a written charge within sixty days from the first hearing on charge. That second clock has a different trigger.
10 | The charge is read and explained, and the plea is recorded. A guilty plea may lead to conviction under Section Two Fifty Two. Arjun contests the charge.
# 4 · PROSECUTION EVIDENCE
10 | The prosecution must prove its case. Sections Two Fifty Three and Two Fifty Four govern its evidence: witnesses testify, and documents are presented.
8 | Arjun's case: an eyewitness describes the incident, a doctor explains medical findings, and recordings may be produced.
8 | Your turn: cross examine. Could the witness really see clearly? Does the medical evidence actually support the account? || Your turn: cross-examine. Could the witness really see clearly? Does the medical evidence actually support the account?
6 | And never forget this: an allegation in a charge sheet is not itself proof of guilt.
10 | What about delay? Section Three Forty Six generally requires day to day hearings while attending witnesses remain to be examined. || What about delay? Section 346 generally requires day-to-day hearings while attending witnesses remain to be examined.
# 5 · THE ACCUSED EXPLAINS
8 | Prosecution witnesses over? Before the defence stage comes a crucial safeguard, under Section Three Fifty One.
8 | It requires the court to put the circumstances appearing against the accused to him, for his explanation.
10 | A witness places Arjun at the scene? He must get a fair opportunity to answer. This is not cross examination on oath. It is his chance to explain. || A witness places Arjun at the scene? He must get a fair opportunity to answer. This is not cross-examination on oath. It is his chance to explain.
10 | Every incriminating circumstance must be put to him specifically. The Supreme Court treats this safeguard as no formality. So check the record.
# 6 · ACQUITTAL OR DEFENCE
10 | Is there any evidence that he did it? Section Two Fifty Five is the checkpoint. No evidence that the accused committed the offence? The Judge must acquit.
8 | Otherwise, Section Two Fifty Six allows defence evidence. Arjun might call a workplace witness, or produce attendance records.
10 | Arjun need not enter the witness box; Section Three Fifty Three lets him testify only on his own written request. Proving guilt remains the prosecution's burden.
# 7 · FINAL ARGUMENTS
8 | Evidence done? Section Two Fifty Seven provides for final arguments. The prosecutor sums up, and the defence replies.
8 | The prosecution links the evidence to the offence. The defence points out weaknesses, inconsistencies or reasonable doubt.
8 | Tie every argument to the record, because a lawyer's argument is not, by itself, proof of a disputed fact.
# 8 · JUDGMENT AND SENTENCE
10 | Arguments are over. How long can the judgment take? Under Section Two Fifty Eight, the Sessions Judge must deliver it within thirty days of the arguments ending.
8 | That period can be extended to forty five days, but only with written reasons. Note the exact date the arguments end.
8 | The court decides whether Arjun's guilt has been proved beyond reasonable doubt, and gives reasons for acquittal or conviction.
8 | Common mistake: these thirty or forty five days start after arguments. They set no deadline for the whole case from the F I R. || Common mistake: these 30 or 45 days start after arguments. They set no deadline for the whole case from the FIR.
8 | If Arjun is convicted, Section Two Fifty Eight, sub section Two, requires a hearing on sentence before punishment.
10 | Come prepared. The court considers the sentencing material and the parties' submissions, then passes the lawful sentence. Any appeal is a separate process.
# 9 · MAGISTRATE TRIALS
8 | Magistrate trial? The provisions change. Warrant cases on a police report follow Sections Two Sixty One to Two Sixty Six.
8 | Complaint warrant cases follow Sections Two Sixty Seven to Two Seventy, with evidence taken before the charge.
10 | Both conclude under Sections Two Seventy One to Two Seventy Three. Summons cases follow Sections Two Seventy Four to Two Seventy Eight, with no formal charge.
# 10 · YOUR TRIAL CHECKLIST
10 | Remember the main Sessions route: charge and plea, prosecution evidence, the accused's explanation, any defence evidence, arguments and judgment.
8 | And sentencing follows only if there is a conviction. Always check the case type, and each deadline's exact starting point.
