VIDEO: CRIMINAL APPEALS AND REVISIONS (BNSS)
==============================================================================

A criminal court has convicted Ravi. He believes the court misunderstood the evidence. In another case, Meera believes a lower court acted beyond its legal powers. Can both simply ask for another trial? The law provides different remedies: appeal and revision. And choosing the correct remedy, court and deadline matters. Under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, or B N S S, let us understand the difference through these two examples. Section Four Thirteen says an appeal exists only where the law provides one. Under Section Four Fifteen, an ordinary conviction by a Magistrate generally goes to the Sessions Court. A conviction by a Sessions Judge or Additional Sessions Judge goes to the High Court. So Ravi's Magistrate conviction ordinarily takes him to Sessions. But guilty pleas and petty cases have restrictions under Sections Four Sixteen and Four Seventeen. Always check whether the particular decision is appealable. An appeal can challenge findings on facts and law, within the permitted scope. Suppose Ravi says the eyewitness could not have identified him in darkness. The appellate court can examine the trial record and his grounds. Section Four Twenty Seven allows different outcomes: dismissing the appeal, reversing the conviction, or ordering a retrial. It may also alter the finding or the sentence, within statutory limits. An appeal is a real opportunity to challenge the decision, but success still depends on the case. Take ordinary conviction appeals, other than death sentences. The usual filing period is thirty days to Sessions and sixty days to the High Court, from the sentence or order. These periods come from Article One Fifteen of the Limitation Act, not from a general B N S S deadline. An application for criminal revision ordinarily has ninety days, under Article One Thirty One. Acquittal appeals, victim appeals, death sentences, Supreme Court proceedings and special statutes need their own limitation checks. Count the deadline carefully. Section Twelve of the Limitation Act excludes the relevant starting day. It also excludes the time legally required to obtain specified copies. So apply for the certified copy promptly, and preserve the dates. If Ravi files late, Section Five allows the court to condone the delay when sufficient cause is shown. That requires the court's decision. Delay is not automatically excused simply because an appeal has been prepared or filed. Ravi files his appeal. Does his prison sentence automatically stop? No. Under Section Four Thirty, the appellate court may suspend execution of the sentence, giving written reasons. It may release a confined appellant on bail or on his own bond. For offences punishable with death, life imprisonment, or ten years or more, the Public Prosecutor must first get a chance to object in writing. If Ravi was on bail and got three years or less, the trial court must grant him bail to file his appeal, unless there are special reasons. So Ravi should seek the necessary interim order. Keep two questions separate. Should the conviction be overturned? And should execution of the sentence be suspended while the appeal is pending? Revision is different. Section Four Thirty Eight lets the Sessions Judge or High Court examine a lower criminal court's record. It checks correctness, legality, propriety and procedural regularity. Sections Four Forty and Four Forty Two set out the revisional powers. Suppose Meera challenges an order because the court lacked the legal authority to make it. If the revision is maintainable, it may address that defect. It is a discretionary supervisory remedy, not a routine second appeal for arguing the entire evidence again. Three limits matter. Section Four Thirty Eight bars revision against interlocutory orders. The Supreme Court has explained that an order's legal character matters, not merely when it was made. A person who chooses Sessions revision cannot then bring another revision before the High Court. And Section Four Forty Two generally prevents a party from using revision after leaving an available appeal unfiled. The revisional court also cannot directly turn an acquittal into a conviction. Remember, revision is not a shortcut around appeal restrictions. Appeals are not only for convicted persons. The proviso to Section Four Thirteen gives a victim a right of appeal. That covers an acquittal, conviction for a lesser offence, or inadequate compensation. For example, an injured victim may challenge an acquittal through the appropriate appellate court. Section Four Eighteen separately concerns government appeals against an inadequate sentence. And Section Four Nineteen deals with specified appeals against acquittal. Who is appealing, and what is being challenged, determines the route and any permission required. One final timeline. Section Four Fifteen, sub section Four, requires certain appeals to be disposed of within six months of filing. These are appeals against sentences under specified sexual offence provisions of the B N S. This is not a six-month deadline for every appeal or revision. Remember: an appeal challenges a decision within the statutory right; revision provides a narrower supervisory check. Identify the remedy, the court and the limitation period early. Obtain the copies, prepare clear grounds, and seek a separate interim order whenever necessary.

VIDEO: REGULAR, ANTICIPATORY AND DEFAULT BAIL (BNSS)
==============================================================================

Three clients call you today. One is already in custody. One fears arrest. And one stays detained while investigators miss their deadline. Which bail do you seek for each? Under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, or B N S S, the situation decides the route: regular, anticipatory or default bail. Client in custody? Regular bail usually means seeking release after arrest, or after surrender into the court's custody. Bailable offence? Section Four Seventy Eight generally makes release a right, once its requirements are met. Non-bailable offence? Section 480 governs bail before the Magistrate, subject to restrictions and exceptions. Section Four Eighty Three gives bail powers to the Sessions Court and the High Court. And remember this: non bailable does not mean bail is impossible. What does the court weigh? The accusation, the available material, and risks such as absconding or influencing witnesses. Take Arjun, arrested in a non bailable case. You seek regular bail. He has a fixed address, he will attend court, and he will not contact witnesses. The prosecution explains its custody concerns. The court then weighs all the circumstances and the legal restrictions. Bail granted? Then Arjun must meet the release requirements and obey every condition. Remember, bail does not decide guilt. No arrest yet, but your client fears one? Anticipatory bail is protection before arrest. Under Section Four Eighty Two, someone who reasonably apprehends arrest for a non bailable offence may approach the Sessions Court or the High Court. The court can direct that, if arrested on that accusation, your client be released on bail. Common mistake alert! Merely filing the application gives your client no automatic protection. The court's order does. Watch the exclusions. Section Four Eighty Two, sub section Four, excludes accusations under B N S Section Sixty Five and Section Seventy, sub section Two. These are specified sexual offences against minor girls. And some special laws also bar anticipatory bail, so check them first. How do you protect Meera? She faces a non bailable accusation and has concrete reasons to fear arrest. She applies under Section Four Eighty Two. Protection granted? The court may require her to attend police questioning, avoid threatening witnesses, and take the court's permission before leaving India. Anticipatory bail protects liberty while the case proceeds. It does not cancel the F I R, or excuse cooperation with a lawful investigation. Route three runs on a clock. Default bail, also called statutory bail, arises under Section One Eighty Seven, sub section Three. It applies when the charge sheet is not filed within the applicable period of detention during investigation. The section specifies ninety days for offences punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment for a term of ten years or more. For other offences, the section specifies sixty days. Your client must claim the right, and be prepared to furnish bail. Before you pick the deadline, check three things: the exact offence, the judicial interpretation and any special law. Where does the clock start? It does not start at the F I R date. It starts at the first remand order, and that day counts as day one. That is the Supreme Court's counting rule. And moving from police custody to judicial custody does not restart the clock. These 60 or 90 days concern detention during investigation. The trial need not end within them. Prolonged undertrial detention has separate rules. Count with me. Ravi's case is in the 60-day category, with no special law extension. His first remand is on 1 January 2026. Mark that as day one. January accounts for 31 days. February adds 28, taking the count to 59. 1 March is day 60. No police report filed by then? The right arises on 2 March, day 61. Ravi can then claim default bail and offer to furnish bail. But he does not simply walk out: he needs a court order and compliance with the release requirements. Timing matters. Who moves first, your claim or the charge sheet? Suppose Ravi claims default bail as soon as the period expires. Right then, no charge sheet has been filed, and he offers to furnish bail. A later charge sheet cannot defeat his claim, just because the court has not decided it yet. But if a valid charge sheet is filed before he claims the right, default bail is ordinarily unavailable. He may still seek regular bail. Be very clear: default bail rests on the missed statutory deadline. It is not an acquittal, and the investigation may continue after release. Three questions to remember. Already in custody? Consider regular bail. Reasonably fearing arrest? Consider anticipatory bail. Investigation deadline expired without the required report? Examine default bail immediately. Know the stage, check the section, act in time!

VIDEO: COGNIZABLE AND NON-COGNIZABLE OFFENCES (BNSS)
==============================================================================

Two people walk into the same police station. Ravi reports a stolen phone. Meena reports an ordinary incident of hurt. Both describe offences, but what follows may be very different. The reason lies in two terms: cognizable and non cognizable. Confuse them, and you misread what the police can and cannot do. Let us understand both through everyday examples. The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, or B N S S, defines cognizable offences in Section Two, sub section One, clause G. In a cognizable offence, police may arrest without a warrant, subject to law. An arrest warrant is a court's written authority. Clause O defines non cognizable offences. Here, police have no such authority merely because that offence is alleged. Where does the classification come from? Check the First Schedule or the applicable special law. Never guess it from how serious an incident sounds. Ravi puts his phone, worth twenty five thousand rupees, beside him on a park bench. While he looks away, a stranger dishonestly takes it. That is cognizable theft under Section Three Hundred Three of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, or B N S. Remember: B N S describes the offence. B N S S governs the procedure. Ravi's information is recorded under B N S S Section One Seventy Three. Section One Seventy Five, sub section One, permits police investigation without a prior Magistrate's order. They can gather evidence, including C C T V footage and witness accounts. Now Meena. During a private argument, an adult neighbour slaps her and causes pain. Assume this is only ordinary voluntarily causing hurt, without a weapon, grievous injury or any additional offence. That is B N S Section One Fifteen, sub section Two, and the B N S S First Schedule classifies it as non cognizable. The police should still record her information. Section One Seventy Four, sub section One, requires recording and referral to the Magistrate. Under sub section Two, police need the competent Magistrate's order before investigating that non cognizable case. Suppose the Magistrate permits investigation into Meena's complaint. Police can now collect evidence. But that order does not itself authorise arrest without a warrant. Section One Seventy Four, sub section Three, preserves this difference. Permission to investigate and authority to arrest are separate. Obtaining the first does not automatically provide the second. Investigation permission is not arrest permission. Now return to Ravi's case. Must police arrest someone immediately because this theft is cognizable? No. Section Thirty Five regulates arrest without a warrant, and its legal conditions must be satisfied. Registering an F I R is not an automatic arrest instruction. In January 2026, the Supreme Court held that for offences punishable with up to seven years, arrest is the exception. Notice to appear under Section Thirty Five, sub section Three, is the rule. Officers must justify arrest under the legal safeguards. Investigation and arrest are different decisions, and an allegation is not a finding of guilt. What if one incident includes a cognizable theft and a separate non cognizable hurt? Section One Seventy Four, sub section Four: where at least one offence is cognizable, the case is treated as cognizable. Police need not split that case into two investigation routes merely because one alleged offence is non cognizable. Another common confusion concerns bail. Cognizable and non cognizable concern police powers and procedure. Bailable and non bailable concern bail. These are separate classifications, shown in separate columns of the First Schedule. A cognizable offence can be bailable. And non bailable does not mean bail can never be granted. Always check the exact offence and the applicable law, rather than treating these words as interchangeable. So what should your client do when reporting an offence? Explain the facts clearly, and preserve the evidence. For cognizable information, Section One Seventy Three allows reporting irrespective of where the offence occurred. This supports what is commonly called Zero F I R. Ask for the free copy provided by sub section Two. If recording is refused, sub section Four lets the informant write to the Superintendent of Police. Where necessary, the next step is an application to the Magistrate. A purely non cognizable case follows the Section One Seventy Four route: a different procedure, not the absence of a remedy. Remember two questions. Can police investigate without a prior Magistrate's order? And what authority is needed to arrest? Cognizable cases generally permit police investigation without that prior order; non cognizable cases require it. Arrest still has its own safeguards. Check Sections Two, Thirty Five, One Seventy Three, One Seventy Four and One Seventy Five. Read them with the First Schedule. The right classification helps you understand the right procedure.

VIDEO: COMPOUNDING AND PLEA BARGAINING (BNSS)
==============================================================================

Ravi and Mohan settle after a neighbourhood fight. In another case, Rohan wants to accept responsibility. He wants a lawful reduction in punishment. Are these the same thing? Under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, or B N S S, these are two different procedures: compounding and plea bargaining. One can have the effect of an acquittal. The other is supervised by the court, and may end in conviction with a reduced sentence. Section Three Fifty Nine lists the offences that can be compounded, and who may compound each one. The person named in the table must agree. Suppose Ravi causes Mohan simple hurt, charged only under B N S Section One Fifteen, sub section Two. The first table lets Mohan, the injured person, compound that offence without the court's permission needed for the second table. The compromise must still be placed before the court. It is a legal process, not merely a handshake. Now change the example. Ravi is charged with voluntarily causing grievous hurt under B N S Section One Seventeen, sub section Two. That offence appears in the second table, so Mohan's agreement also needs the court's permission. Section Three Fifty Nine, sub section Eight, says lawful compounding has the effect of an acquittal of the accused concerned. Simple and grievous hurt have different compounding requirements. Always check the exact charge. Section Three Fifty Nine sets no general thirty day or sixty day deadline for compounding. But the stage of the case matters. After commitment for trial, or during an appeal after conviction, the court's leave is required under sub section Five. A private settlement cannot make every offence compoundable. Murder, for example, cannot be compounded under this section. Special statutes can have their own rules. Before promising a compromise will end a case, check every charge and the necessary court process. Plea bargaining has a separate eligibility test under Section Two Eighty Nine. It excludes offences punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment exceeding seven years. It also excludes offences against women or children, and notified offences affecting the country's socio-economic condition. Section Three Hundred excludes juvenile or child accused from this chapter. An earlier conviction for the same offence is another obstacle, under Section Two Ninety. So Rohan must qualify before any talk of compensation or punishment can begin. Assume Rohan is an adult accused in an eligible property case. Under Section Two Ninety, sub section One, he can apply to the trial court within thirty days from framing of the charge. The application briefly describes the case, and includes his sworn affidavit. It confirms that he acts voluntarily, understands the punishment, and has no previous conviction for the same offence. The clock is tied to framing the charge. It does not begin simply because the F I R was registered or Rohan was arrested. The judge examines Rohan privately, with the other party absent, to check that his application is voluntary. If satisfied, Section Two Ninety, sub section Four, requires the court to give the parties time, not exceeding sixty days. That time is to work out a mutually satisfactory disposition, which may include compensation and expenses for the victim. This is a separate period, allowed by the court. It is not sixty days from arrest. And it does not guarantee that the whole case finishes within ninety days. Section Two Ninety One brings the participants into a voluntary process. In a police report case, these include the prosecutor, the investigating officer, the accused and the victim. Section Two Ninety Two requires a signed report if agreement is reached. If not, proceedings resume from the application stage. Section Two Ninety Nine protects statements or facts in the application from being used for other purposes. Rohan cannot be forced to agree. And filing alone does not complete the process. Section Two Ninety Three provides for victim compensation, and for probation where applicable. It also sets reduced sentences. Take an eligible offence with no minimum sentence, and an accused never previously convicted. The court may impose one sixth of the punishment provided or extendable. If the maximum is three years, that fraction is six months. Where a statutory minimum applies, the first time offender fraction is one fourth of that minimum. These are conditional court decisions, not a guaranteed discount negotiated privately. Under Section Two Ninety Four, the plea bargaining judgment is delivered in open court. Section Two Ninety Five makes it final, with no ordinary appeal, while preserving the specified constitutional remedies. Remember the difference. Lawful compounding has the effect of acquittal. Plea bargaining can result in conviction, with a lawful sentencing benefit. Check eligibility, get independent legal advice, and understand the consequences before agreeing. Neither an informal compromise nor an application alone can replace the required legal process.

VIDEO: POLICE CUSTODY AND JUDICIAL CUSTODY (BNSS)
==============================================================================

Your client is arrested. Police want him for questioning. Will it be police custody, or judicial custody? Who holds him, who authorises it, and for how long? Under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, or B N S S, these are distinct forms of detention, with safeguards. Here is the first deadline. After arrest without warrant, Section Fifty Eight prohibits detention beyond twenty four hours without a Magistrate's authorisation. Necessary travel time to court is excluded, and police must avoid unnecessary delay. Note the exact arrest time. Ravi is arrested on Monday morning. Police cannot keep him at the station for days because questioning is unfinished. If police need further detention, Section One Eighty Seven requires production before a Magistrate, who must consider whether remand is justified. Who holds your client, and why? In police custody, he is entrusted to investigating police, for lawful investigation under the remand order. That can include lawful questioning, or recovering evidence. Judicial custody is different: the accused is ordinarily kept in prison. Jail authorities hold him, on the court's order. The investigating officer does not have the same direct custody. Both forms require legal justification. Neither is a finding of guilt, and judicial custody does not mean a conviction. Is it 15, or 40? Here is the key limit in the ordinary BNSS scheme. Section 187(2), as the Supreme Court reads it, allows 15 police-custody days in all. Each grant still needs the Magistrate's decision. Together or in parts, those days must all fall within the first 40 days of remand in a 60-day case, or the first 60 in a 90-day case. Forty and sixty only describe the window, not the number of days police may keep someone. The Supreme Court confirmed this in July 2026. Test it on Ravi. Assume his case is in the sixty day category, and he stays in custody throughout. The Magistrate grants five days of police custody. Then judicial custody follows. New evidence emerges, and on day 20, the police ask for another five days. Can the Magistrate grant them? Yes, if they are justified, because all five days fall within the first forty days, and only ten of the fifteen would be used. But returning to police custody does not create a fresh fifteen day allowance, or restart the overall remand clock. Check two numbers: days used, and the window. Is a video link enough for police custody? No. Section One Eighty Seven, sub section Four, requires physical production before the Magistrate, each time. For judicial custody, the rule is different: it may be extended through physical or audio-video production. Under sub section Seven, the Magistrate must record reasons for police custody. A police request is not a remand order, so check the order for reasons. And to move Ravi from prison into police custody, investigators need a fresh court order, within the limits. Where do 60 and 90 come from? Section 187(3) sets the investigation detention periods linked to default bail. That is ninety days for offences punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment for ten years or more, and sixty days for other offences. Police and judicial custody both count towards that total. It is not 15 days plus another 60 or 90. Check the exact charge and the applicable judicial interpretation. Special laws can also modify these rules. The period ends, and the required charge sheet is missing. Is your client free? Expiry alone does not open the prison gates. Your client must claim default bail in time, and must be ready to furnish bail. A timely charge sheet can let judicial custody continue, through lawful orders. After cognizance or the start of trial, Section Three Forty Six permits remand when proceedings are postponed or adjourned. That is at most 15 days at a time under that section. Do not confuse it with the 15-day police-custody limit. Custody does not remove basic rights. Section Forty Seven requires the grounds of arrest to be communicated forthwith. Section Forty Eight requires that a nominated relative or friend be informed. Section Thirty Eight gives your client the right to meet an advocate of his choice during interrogation, though not throughout it. Sections Fifty Three and Fifty Six provide for medical examination, and for the arrested person's health and safety. And bail can be sought before the maximum custody periods expire: the court need not use every available day. Remember the two ordinary limits: 24 hours before a Magistrate must authorise detention, and 15 police-custody days in all. All of it sits inside the 40, 60 and 90-day framework. Count the days, check the order, and claim default bail in time.

VIDEO: DISCHARGE AND FRAMING OF CHARGES (BNSS)
==============================================================================

Charge sheet filed? That does not mean a full trial. The court must first screen the case, and that screening is where a sharp advocate can win early. The real question is simple: is there enough material to proceed against your client? If not, discharge. Under the B N S S, two tools do this screening: discharge and framing of charge. Know the court, section and deadline, and you can spare your client an unnecessary trial. What is discharge? The court finds that proceeding against the accused is not justified at this stage. It is not bail, which concerns liberty while the case continues. And it is not an acquittal after trial either. Discharge needs judicial consideration and recorded reasons. A denial alone will not get it, and neither will simply filing an application. Sessions case first. Take an ordinary case committed under Section Two Thirty Two. Section Two Fifty lets the accused apply for discharge within sixty days from commitment. The Judge considers the record and documents, and hears both sides. No sufficient ground for proceeding? The Judge must discharge the accused, and record reasons. But if there is ground to presume an offence only a Sessions Court can try, Section Two Fifty One requires a written charge. That charge must be framed within sixty days from the first hearing on charge. A different starting point! Some High Courts treat the sixty day application window as directory. Even so, never gamble on it. File within time. Different court, different clock. In a Magistrate's warrant case on a police report, Section Two Sixty Two applies. Here the accused may apply for discharge within sixty days from the supply of document copies under Section Two Thirty. The Magistrate considers the police report and papers, and hears both sides. Charge groundless? The Magistrate must discharge, with reasons. If the test for proceeding is met, Section Two Sixty Three requires a written charge within sixty days from the first hearing on charge. This is where advocates slip, so take three dates. A Sessions case is committed on 1 June. That starts its discharge window. In a different case, a Magistrate's warrant case on a police report, copies are supplied on 10 June. That starts its own application window. If the first hearing on charge is on 1 July, the charge-framing period runs from that hearing. Record the correct trigger for each clock: not the arrest, not the F I R, and never one date for every deadline. What does a charge really do? It is the court's formal statement of the offence your client must answer. Sections Two Thirty Four and Two Thirty Five cover its contents: the offence, the law, the time, the place, and the person involved. Check every particular. Under Sections Two Fifty One and Two Sixty Three, the charge is read and explained, and the accused is asked whether he pleads guilty or claims trial. If he claims trial, the case moves into evidence. A charge does not establish guilt. The prosecution must still prove its case. What test applies at this stage? Does the material supply a legally sufficient basis to proceed against your client? Here is the line: strong suspicion founded on the material can justify framing a charge. Proof beyond reasonable doubt belongs to the decision on guilt. The court must apply its mind, but it should not run a mini trial to decide which witness is truthful. Equally, a bare accusation without the required legal ingredients is not enough. So test the material against every ingredient. And the Supreme Court has repeatedly distinguished this screening from deciding guilt. Picture this: Arjun is accused of stealing Ravi's laptop, worth sixty thousand rupees. Look at two possible files. First file: the prosecution papers, even accepted at face value, contain nothing connecting Arjun to the theft. Argue that the charge is groundless, and seek discharge. Second file: a witness account and supporting material link him to it. Then a dispute about the witness's reliability may need evidence at trial. The actual record controls the result, and neither example guarantees an outcome. One more trap: these sixty day clocks do not fit every case. Warrant cases not started on a police report follow Sections Two Sixty Seven to Two Sixty Nine. In summons cases, Section Two Seventy Four generally needs no formal charge. The particulars of the accusation are simply stated to the accused. But if the accusation is groundless, the Magistrate must record reasons and release the accused. That release then has the effect of a discharge. Special courts and special statutes may have additional rules of their own. Check them first. Remember: discharge asks whether the case should proceed. The charge identifies what must be answered. Neither is a finding of guilt. Your checklist: check the case type, record the correct deadline trigger, and raise your discharge grounds promptly. Court moving to frame the charge too early? Ask for time, and put your objection on record.

VIDEO: REJECTION AND RETURN OF PLAINT (CPC ORDER VII)
==============================================================================

You file a civil suit. The judge says this plaint cannot proceed. Is it rejected, or returned? Each leads to a different procedure, and a different remedy. The answer lies in Order Seven of the Code of Civil Procedure, or C P C, and four leading Supreme Court judgments. Two rules, two questions. Rule Ten deals with return: is this the proper court? It commonly applies when territorial or pecuniary jurisdiction is missing. Rule Eleven deals with rejection: does a statutory ground stop the plaint from proceeding? A weak case alone is not enough. Which grounds count? Rule Eleven lists six. First, the plaint discloses no cause of action. Second, the relief is undervalued, and not corrected in the time fixed. Third, the plaint is insufficiently stamped, and the stamp paper is not supplied in time. Fourth, the suit appears, from the statement in the plaint, to be barred by law. Fifth, the plaint is not filed in duplicate. Sixth, the plaintiff fails to comply with Rule Nine. For valuation and stamp defects, the plaintiff first gets time to correct them. How closely must a court read a plaint? In T. Arivandandam v. T.V. Satyapal, the Supreme Court said the plaint must receive a meaningful reading. Clever drafting cannot manufacture a cause of action where none exists. So the court must look past the drafting to the substance. Picture a plaint full of impressive allegations, but missing the material facts that create a right to relief. Drafting a plaint? Plead those material facts. But the court must identify a ground under Rule Eleven. It cannot reject a plaint merely because the case looks difficult. Seeking rejection? Name the ground. What may the court read? In Dahiben v. Arvindbhai Kalyanji Bhanusali, the answer is the plaint as a whole, with the documents it relies on. Acting for the defendant? Your competing version of the facts does not decide a Rule Eleven application. In Dahiben, a sale deed was challenged years later. The Court upheld the rejection. The suit was time-barred, and lacked a sustainable cause of action. Practice point: check the pleaded dates and documents carefully. Limitation supports rejection only when the bar is apparent from the plaint. Acting for the plaintiff? Disputed facts cannot simply be resolved against your client at this stage. The flip side is return. A properly pleaded claim may still be filed in the wrong court. Under Rule Ten, the plaint is returned for presentation to the court where the suit should have been instituted. Return is about the forum, not the merits. The judge endorses the plaint with the dates of presentation and return, the presenter's name, and brief reasons for returning it. Where its conditions apply, Rule 10A can fix an appearance date in the proper court. What happens in the proper court? In ONGC v. Modern Construction, the Supreme Court said the returned plaint is treated as a fresh institution. The trial proceeds afresh. It does not continue the earlier suit, so limitation matters. Section Fourteen of the Limitation Act may exclude the time spent pursuing the case in a court without jurisdiction. But that protection must be established. Return does not preserve the original filing date, so never assume it protects your client. Is a return just a transfer? No. The three-judge bench in EXL Careers v. Frankfinn Aviation Services confirmed it. After a return under Rules 10 and 10A, the proceedings must begin afresh. Rule 10A simplifies appearance and service. It does not turn a return into a transfer that keeps the earlier trial stage. One qualification. In E X L itself, the Supreme Court used its constitutional powers on exceptional facts, and left the High Court's order undisturbed. That relief does not change the general rule. Cite E X L for the rule, not for its result. Rejected? That rejection is deemed a decree under Section Two, sub section Two. So it ordinarily attracts a first appeal under Section Ninety Six. An order returning a plaint is appealable under Order XLIII Rule 1(a), except where the Rule 10A procedure was followed. Rejection does not, by itself, bar a fresh plaint on the same cause of action. Rule Thirteen says so. But a fresh plaint must still overcome limitation and the legal defect. Refiling a barred claim does not remove the bar. Test yourself with two plaints. The first pleads a recoverable debt, but the chosen court lacks territorial jurisdiction. Think return, under Rule Ten. The second plaint, read with its own documents, discloses no cause of action. Think rejection, under Rule Eleven. Before drafting or opposing either application, check these four things: the forum, the material facts, the documents relied on, and limitation. Return sends the plaint to the proper court. Rejection follows a specified statutory ground. Get it right: that distinction decides the next step in the entire case.
